⠿ The Problem

Blind students hunger to read.
The shelves just don't speak their language.

285M

people globally live with visual impairment — 19 million of them are children under 14 (NIH, 2023).

<10%

of all published books are ever converted into any accessible format. Braille fiction for children is a fraction of that.

~5M

Braille readers exist globally, yet most blind school libraries carry fewer than 20 story books in Braille.

⠂ What's Missing
  • Most Braille books in Indian blind schools are decades-old textbooks or religious scriptures — not stories children choose to read.
  • Reading is treated as academic tool for blind students — never as pleasure, never as escape, never as adventure.
  • Fiction and short stories in Braille are seen as "non-essential" — the first thing cut from budgets, the last thing donated.
  • Students frequently share one Braille volume among an entire class, or depend entirely on a teacher reading aloud.
  • Accessible formats are created only after a sighted book succeeds — Braille comes last, if at all.
  • This is not a capability problem. Blind children can and do read. They simply have nothing to read.

⠂ Ways to Write Braille
Slate & Stylus

A handheld tool, usually plastic, used with a stylus to emboss Braille dots onto paper.

How to write using it
  1. Place the paper into the slate and secure it.
  2. Use the stylus to press the dots into the paper.
  3. Since you're writing on the back of the paper, you write from right to left.
  4. Turn the paper over to read the raised dots from left to right.

⠿ Remember: you must write backwards, although you will read forwards.

Brailler

A machine that works like a typewriter for Braille — it embosses raised dots directly onto paper.

How it works
  1. Six keys represent the six dots — 3 2 1 | 4 5 6: left side dots 3, 2, 1; right side dots 4, 5, 6.
  2. Press the keys simultaneously in combination, not individually, to form letters and symbols.
  3. The space bar creates a space between words.
  4. The enter bar is used to begin a new line.

⠂ Braille Grammar & Writing Rules
Capital Letters

A capital indicator — dot 6 — is placed directly before a letter to show that it's capitalised.

Capital A = dot 6 + A

Numbers

Numbers begin with a number sign (dots 3-4-5-6), followed by the Braille patterns for A–J.

A corresponds to 1, and each letter continues accordingly, with J corresponding to 0.

Punctuation

Punctuation is placed directly after the word it belongs to, with no space before it.

Comma ,dots 2
Full stop .dots 2-5-6
Question mark ?dots 2-6
Exclamation mark !dots 2-3-5
Colon :dots 2-5
Semicolon ;dots 2-3
Apostrophe 'dot 3
Hyphen -dots 3-6
Sentence Structure
  • Leave one blank cell between words.
  • Use a capital indicator when a word begins with a capital letter.
  • Place punctuation directly after the word.
  • Use the number sign before numbers.
  • Grade 1 Braille writes words letter by letter, without contractions.

⠂ Tips & Tricks
01

Learn the six dots first: 1 4 / 2 5 / 3 6.

02

Learn letters in groups instead of memorising every letter individually.

03

Keep a dot-number chart nearby when practising or transcribing.

04

Pay attention to punctuation — small mistakes can change the meaning of a sentence.

05

Double-check numbers to make sure the number sign is included.

06

Proofread by reading the Braille itself, not just by comparing it with the original text.

07

Accuracy comes before speed — take your time, especially when transcribing a full book.

⠂ The Magic I Find Behind Reading

Ever since I started reading, I never stopped. I don't mean reading in the everyday sense — texts, signs, the internet — I mean books. Everywhere I went as a child, there was always one in my hand, because books carried me somewhere far more serene than the world I was actually standing in.

⠂ My Love For Writing

My first book was written when I was four — terrible illustrations, twenty spelling mistakes, and a passion I couldn't yet explain. I kept writing all through childhood and my teenage years, and that love only grew. I loved how a story could carry meaning without saying it outright, how it could share what I wanted the world to know, and how it let me become more of who I am. At thirteen, I published my first novel, Eunoia of the Endless Horizon, available on Amazon worldwide — and I'm nowhere near done writing.

⠂ Where My Passion For Braille Began

At some point I decided I wanted to learn Braille, so I did. It started almost on a whim, but the dedication that followed surprised even me — I spent hours, days, memorising the number codes, the letters, how they looked, how they felt under my fingers. I learned to type Braille on a slate and a Brailler, and the first thing I ever transcribed was my own book of poetry.

⠿ Where Between the Dots Started

Over time — through research, through paying attention to the world around me — it became clear that visually impaired children simply don't have enough access to creative writing. I wanted them to feel the same spark I did with fiction as a child. So I brought together everything I loved — writing, Braille, and the belief that every child deserves a story — and built Between the Dots: a way to bring reading to those who want it, but haven't had the chance.
